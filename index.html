How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs. Each new row is one peg wider than the row above it, and the ball faces a left-or-right choice at every contact point. After crossing all the rows, it lands in one of the bottom pockets, which are always one more than the row count.

Because every bounce is a coin-flip style deflection, the middle pockets fill most often and the edge pockets appear far less often. Statistically, the layout behaves like a Galton board and follows a binomial curve. In plain terms, common centre landings pay less, while rare edge landings pay more.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for the finished ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without shifting the long-run return much.
House edge
The small difference between true odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players check results after the round.
Variance
The size of the swings around the average result, which is the main thing the risk setting changes.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each bounce comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify that the result was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes increase while their chance drops. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically better. Low risk smooths the ride, high risk concentrates value into rare edge hits. The best option depends on your budget and how much swing you are comfortable with.
